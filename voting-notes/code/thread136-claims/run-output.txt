C1a  A candidate who loses every matchup never changes the 1st-degree
     result -- Sass's '+1 win to everyone' argument, tested
==========================================================================
  6,631 profiles where X loses all four matchups: the 1st-degree
  outcome is identical with and without X, every time.
  Reason it is a theorem, not luck: X's presence adds exactly +1 to every
  other candidate's Copeland score, so the ranking by wins -- and hence the
  finalist set -- is untouched; X is never a finalist, so the margins summed
  among finalists never see it.

==========================================================================
C1b  The same all-losing candidate can flip the 2nd-degree tiebreak
==========================================================================
  Six ballots (truncation and equal ranks both in play):
    A
    C > B > D
    C > A > B > X=D
    C > B > D > A
    A > X > C > D > B
    D=A > X > B > C
  Copeland: A 3.5, B 2.0, C 3.5, D 1.0, X 0.0   -> X loses all four matchups, is never a finalist
  Finalists ['A', 'C'], and they are pairwise tied (3-3), so the 1st degree cannot separate them.
  2nd degree, margins summed over all candidates:
    without X:  A +3, C +6   -> C
    with X:     A +8, C +7   -> A
    the whole difference is the margin against X: A beats X by 5, C by only 1.
  A candidate who wins nothing decides the election. This is the case Sass
  himself flagged in post 28 -- confirmed.
  Rate: 176 flips in 21,980 random profiles where X loses every
  matchup (0.80%) -- rare, and it needs the 1st degree to have
  tied first, which is itself the rare case (see C4).

==========================================================================
C1c  A candidate with 2% of first preferences flips the winner
==========================================================================
  100 ballots:  49 A>B>C   2 C>A>B   49 B>C>A
    A vs B: 51-49   A vs C: 49-51   B vs C: 98-2
  Without C: A beats B 51-49  ->  winner A
  With C:    3-cycle, all three tie on 1 win ['A', 'B', 'C'], margins A 0, B +94, C -94  ->  winner B
  C takes 2 first preferences and loses to B by 96 votes. It is 'weak' by
  any ordinary meaning -- but it beats A, so it does NOT hand every
  contender the same +1, and the winner changes. Sass's argument covers
  only candidates who lose to everyone; the spoilers that matter are
  exactly the ones that don't. (Every Condorcet method fails here -- this
  is IIA failure, not a Ranked Robin defect.)

==========================================================================
C2  'Sum every candidate's margins over all others' IS Borda
==========================================================================
  Identity holds on 20,000 random profiles, with equal ranks and
  truncation switched on:
      sum of x's margins  ==  2 * Borda(x)  -  (m-1) * V
  The subtracted term is the same for every candidate, so the two rankings
  are identical, not merely correlated. Post 40's one-line reply is exactly
  right -- and stays right under equal ranking and truncation, provided
  Borda is scored tournament-style (half a point per pairwise tie, unranked
  tied last). Waugh's simplification deletes the Copeland gate, and the
  gate is the only thing separating Ranked Robin from Borda.

C3  The one-sentence pitch is equivalent only under two conventions
    the sentence does not state
==========================================================================
  (i) Rank gaps. 100 ballots, two candidates:
        51 voters mark A 1st, B 2nd
        49 voters mark B 1st and A 9th (ranks 2-8 skipped)
      Method (skipped ranks ignored): A beats B 51-49, elects A
      Average rank as the voter WROTE it:  A 4.92, B 1.51  -> B looks better
      Average rank with gaps collapsed:    A 1.49, B 1.51  -> A, matching the method
      A voter who takes 'best average rank' literally gets the opposite
      winner. Sass's objection in post 18 was correct.

  (ii) Which election is the average taken over? Ballots:
       C > A > B > D
       C > A > B > D
       D > B > A > C
       C > B > A > D
       B > D > C > A
       D > A > C > B
       D > B > C > A
       A > B > D > C
       A > B > C > D
      Finalists tied on wins: ['A', 'B']
      Average rank over the FULL ballot: A 2.44, B 2.33  -> B
      Margins among the finalists only (the actual rule) -> A
      Same sentence, two readings, two winners. The equivalence
      holds only when the average is taken within the finalist set,
      which is what post 18's 'among' has to carry on its own.

C4  Tie rates against electorate size, 4 candidates, impartial culture
==========================================================================
   voters  trials  no Condorcet w  Copeland tied  after 1st deg  after 2nd deg
        5   20000         13.70%        13.70%         4.81%         2.51%
       15   20000         16.50%        16.50%         2.12%         0.66%
       51   12000         17.05%        17.05%         1.20%         0.27%
      201    5000         17.22%        17.22%         0.76%         0.00%
     1001    1500         16.73%        16.73%         0.20%         0.00%
  The first two columns are equal in every row, and that is forced, not luck:
  with m candidates and no pairwise ties, the scores sum to m(m-1)/2, so a
  unique top score of m-2 needs m^2-3m+1 >= m(m-1)/2, i.e. m >= 5. For four
  or fewer candidates, 'no Condorcet winner' and 'Copeland tie' are the same
  event -- every cycle reaches the margins rung. From five candidates up they
  come apart:
    m=5, 101 voters: no Condorcet winner 24.0%; of those, 14.5% still have a
      unique Copeland winner, so the tiebreaker never runs at all.
    m=6, 101 voters: no Condorcet winner 32.0%; of those, 25.8% still have a
      unique Copeland winner, so the tiebreaker never runs at all.
  Copeland ties stay common at every size -- they are structural (cycles and
  symmetric standoffs), not an artifact of small electorates. The margins
  rung is what melts away with V: it needs an exact integer coincidence,
  which gets rarer as the numbers get bigger. Waugh's post-13 intuition,
  confirmed: the extra information Ranked Robin keeps is exactly what
  converts a persistent tie rate into a vanishing one.
