# RCV (IRV) and "Core Support"

Source: [RCV and core support](https://voting-in-the-abstract.medium.com/rcv-and-core-support-e0d1780a9184) — Marcus Ogren, *Voting in the Abstract*

## What it's about

The 2022 Alaska special election for the U.S. House: Nick Begich (R) was the Condorcet winner — he beat Mary Peltola (D) head-to-head and Sarah Palin (R) by a wide margin — yet IRV eliminated him first and Peltola won. IRV advocates like Steven Hill defend that outcome by arguing a winner needs both **core support** (many first rankings) and **broad support** (many backup rankings), while Condorcet methods only demand broad support. Ogren, a Condorcet/STAR supporter, examines whether "core support" is a coherent concept at all.

## Key takeaways

- **Core support ≠ first-place rankings.** How many first-place rankings a candidate gets depends on who else is running (Begich has more first-choice support than Peltola with Palin out of the race, less with her in it). Defining core support that way bakes the spoiler effect into your values: whether A would represent voters better than B can't logically depend on whether C is also a candidate.
- **Ordinal vs. cardinal.** Ordinal preferences (rankings) can't be what we care about *intrinsically* — only cardinal preferences (how much a voter likes each candidate) can define core support coherently. A voter who loves both Republicans but narrowly prefers Palin is still a core Begich supporter; a Democrat who merely tolerates Begich is not.
- **Proof-of-concept cardinal methods** that reward both kinds of support (on 0–5 star ballots):
  1. *Multiplicative:* core = sum of cubed scores, broad = sum of square roots of scores, elect the highest core × broad product. Extra broad support matters most for candidates short on it, and vice versa.
  2. *Threshold:* eliminate everyone given 5 stars on <30% of ballots, then pick the Condorcet winner among the rest (Smith//Score if there's a cycle).
- **IRV doesn't even serve its own standard well.** It ignores some preferences outright — Peltola voters' Begich-over-Palin preference never enters the count. In an alternate scenario where Palin and Begich each had ~2.7% more first-choice support, Palin wins under IRV even though Begich clears the actual election's core-support bar and crushes her head-to-head.
- **The real values difference.** Under Condorcet or STAR, opposing voters' ballots cancel symmetrically, so candidates have equal incentive to appeal to every voter. Under IRV, a committed opponent's lower rankings often never count, so candidates can ignore voters who will never rank them first. That asymmetry is the *goal* of demanding core support, not a side effect. Ogren's stance: equal incentives reduce polarization and reward candidates who reach across the aisle.
- **Against the "bland winner" objection.** Condorcet doesn't ignore core support — being ranked #1 still beats #2; core and broad support are just fungible rather than core being a hard requirement. Empirically the 2016 "squeezed centrist" was Bernie Sanders, not the blander Gary Johnson, so blandness didn't pay. And winning as "everyone's second choice" needs three-plus mutually hostile factions — mathematically possible, sociologically a stretch.
- **A bullet Ogren bites:** if preference intensity matters, then sometimes the candidate with fewer supporters in a *two-way* race is still the rightful winner.
- **Factual correction:** Condorcet is not unused in practice — the Schulze method is used by several European cities and political parties.

## New ideas and terms (glossary)

- **Core support** — enthusiasm-based backing. Hill treats it as "many first rankings"; Ogren argues it's only coherent as a *cardinal* notion: how strongly a voter likes the candidate, independent of who else is running.
- **Broad support** — acceptability to a wide swath of voters (high backup rankings / decent scores from many people).
- **Ordinal preferences** — rankings only ("Palin > Begich > Peltola"); say nothing about gaps between candidates.
- **Cardinal preferences** — strength of preference ("Palin 5, Begich 4, Peltola 0"); what utility-style arguments need.
- **Condorcet winner** — the candidate who beats every other candidate head-to-head. A method that always elects them (when one exists) is a Condorcet method.
- **Condorcet cycle / Smith//Score** — head-to-head results can cycle (A>B>C>A); Smith//Score breaks the tie by highest score within the smallest unbeaten set.
- **Center squeeze** — failure mode of IRV where a broadly acceptable middle candidate is eliminated early because first-choice votes split to the flanks (Begich in Alaska 2022).
- **Spoiler effect in your *values*** — the article's sharpest idea: it's not just that methods can suffer spoilers; a *definition* of merit (core support = first rankings) can itself be spoiler-dependent, which makes it incoherent as a value.
- **Fungibility of support** — under Condorcet, extra broad support can compensate for less core support (and vice versa); under IRV, insufficient first-choice support is an absolute veto and remaining broad support is discarded.
- **Core × broad method** (proof of concept) — score ballots; core = Σ score³, broad = Σ √score; elect the max product. Cubing rewards enthusiasm, square-rooting rewards mere acceptability, multiplying demands both.
- **Threshold method** (proof of concept) — require 5-star ratings on ≥30% of ballots to qualify, then Condorcet among qualifiers.
- **Top Two IRV** — only the two first-choice leaders reach the runoff. Worse than IRV even by core-support logic, because some second-choice support *is* core support (a Palin-first Republican may be a genuine core Begich supporter).
- **Strategic straightforwardness** — how easy it is for voters to vote honestly without regretting it; an axis where IRV scores well against these cardinal constructions.
- **Equal incentives argument** — the article's positive case: methods where opposing ballots cancel symmetrically (Condorcet, STAR) give candidates a reason to court *every* voter, which dampens polarization; demanding core support deliberately breaks that symmetry.
- **Arrow's theorem caveat** — the impossibility results apply to voting *methods*; Ogren's argument is about an idealized preference-aggregation standard used to judge methods, so it doesn't contradict Arrow.

## The one-question summary

Should politicians care mainly about their core supporters, or about all voters equally? If the former, IRV expresses your values; if the latter, Condorcet or STAR does.
