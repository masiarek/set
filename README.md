Also why the ranked *ballot* is separable from the tabulation *rule*: six states already mail ranked ballots to military and overseas voters for runoffs while using no IRV at all, and truncated ballots — which UOCAVA voters are likeliest to return — exhaust under IRV but merely abstain from a few matchups under Ranked Robin. Notes: [voting-notes/ranked-robin-origins.md](voting-notes/ranked-robin-origins.md)
- The origin thread's claims, checked — the four assertions in topic 136 nobody ever ran numbers against. Sass's "adding a weak candidate changes nothing" is a theorem for candidates who lose *every* matchup and false otherwise: a candidate with 2% of first preferences flips the winner, and a six-ballot election is decided by a candidate who wins nothing. "Sum all margins = Borda" is an exact identity that survives equal ranks and truncation, so the Copeland gate is the only thing between Ranked Robin and Borda. The "best average rank" one-liner elects the other candidate under either of two conventions it never states. Ties do thin out with electorate size — but Copeland's own rate stays flat at ~17%, and with ≤4 candidates "no Condorcet winner" and "Copeland tie" are provably the same event, so the margins rung is the entire cycle path in normal-sized fields. - [Approval voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approval_voting) (Wikipedia) — the simplest cardinal method, and the only one whose criterion compliance is a property of *how voters set their cutoff* rather than of the tabulation: four rows in the compliance table, one per voter model, and only fully dichotomous preferences pass everything. Working the Tennessee zero-info scenario by hand elects the Condorcet *loser* on minority approval; the dichotomous-cutoff example isolates the IIA failure to the cutoff rule alone. Keeps the retreats advocacy pages omit — IEEE 2002, Dartmouth 82–18 in 2009, the Independent Party of Oregon leaving for STAR in 2020, North Dakota's April 2025 ban ending Fargo — because universal bullet voting collapses approval into plurality, and 79% bullet voted even in the 1987 MAA election run by the mathematicians who chose the method. Notes: [voting-notes/approval-voting.md](voting-notes/approval-voting.md)
- [STAR voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STAR_voting) (Wikipedia) — a thin, advocacy-sourced article carrying a third-party-sources banner, whose criterion failures are asserted with no examples, so they're built and checked locally instead. One 100-voter profile fails majority, Condorcet and clone independence at once: the strict favourite of 52 of 100, and the Condorcet winner, never reaches the runoff — and deleting the front-runner's clone flips the winner back. Plus a later-no-harm failure where scoring a second choice 4 instead of 0 costs that bloc its own favourite, and a favorite-betrayal failure where exhaustive search over all 216 ballots proves equal-rating won't save you. mono-raise survives 196,699 random profiles; Woodall's mono-raise-delete doesn't. Two arithmetic slips found in the article itself. Notes: [voting-notes/star-voting.md](voting-notes/star-voting.md), verifier: [voting-notes/code/star-voting/verify.py](voting-notes/code/star-voting/verify.py)
- Our strategy pages vs. [Wikipedia's "Strategic voting"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_voting#Cardinal_single-winner_voting) — an audit of the starvoting.org/equal.vote strategy education chain against the encyclopedia article, with draft replacement copy. The arguments hold; the vocabulary, the sourcing, and two of the claims don't. Our four "insincerity" categories match nothing in the literature (nobody searching "burial" finds a heading called *Weak Insincerity*), "Peer Reviewed studies" are invoked twice and named zero times while equal.vote/peer_review names two real journal papers, and "STAR never encourages strong insincerity" is refuted by the exhaustive-search counterexample in our own star-voting.md — where Farewell to Pass/Fail is the answer we already have and don't deploy. Two further verified counterexamples: STAR fails the participation criterion, and at the score cut burial and boosting are the *same move* — a 7-voter profile where a strategist who already has the frontrunner at 0 lifts a third candidate over them instead, and wins, because the "your favorite gets squeezed out" rebuttal only binds when your favorite is marginal. - [Score voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Score_voting) (Wikipedia) — the baseline the other two cardinal methods are defined against, and the note that explains why they exist: **honest score voting is one of the best methods there is, and strategic score voting is plurality.** On the article's own Tennessee example sincere scores elect Nashville, the Condorcet winner, 603–457; min-max the same preferences and the same electorate elects Memphis 42–41, the Condorcet *loser*. Score is also the only method here that satisfies IIA — but only under absolute scoring; let voters renormalise to the field and dropping a loser flips the winner. Three further findings: the article's lead says highest *average* wins while its example computes *totals*, which elect different candidates the moment a ballot has a blank (Pirate Party Bavaria really does use average-with-quorum); the same historical elections are claimed as approval on one Wikipedia page and score on the other; and this article's 0–10 table finally explains the STAR table discrepancy — it's the same table halved with round-half-to-even, so `5.378 → 5 → 2.5 → 2` where deriving 0–5 directly gives 3. Double rounding, not a typo. Notes: [voting-notes/score-voting.md](voting-notes/score-voting.md), verifier: [voting-notes/code/score-voting/verify.py](voting-notes/code/score-voting/verify.py)
- [Majority judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_judgment) (Wikipedia) — Balinski and Laraki's answer to the sum-vs-average question: use the **median**. One profile shows exactly what that buys — a bloc min-maxing swings score totals from 203–203 to 143–223 and moves neither median at all. What it costs is proved rather than observed: B&L's own theorem says the only methods satisfying participation plus continuity are point-summing (score-like) ones, so MJ's no-show paradox is forced, not fixable, and they proved it themselves. Laslier's critique verified in full: on a 650-voter left–right axis, five candidates tie at median "good" and **the highest-*median* rule elects Left while the Condorcet winner and the score winner are both Center** — it hands the election to the larger homogeneous wing, so it fails the *median voter* criterion. Also a load-bearing clause in the Tennessee example: drop "the farthest city gets Poor", keep the mileage bands, and MJ elects Memphis, the Condorcet loser, on a five-mile technicality. MJ disagrees with plain score 16.2% of the time and misses the Condorcet winner 7.9% — against STAR's 1.6%, so swapping the mean for the median is a far bigger change than adding a runoff. Notes: [voting-notes/majority-judgment.md](voting-notes/majority-judgment.md), verifier: [voting-notes/code/majority-judgment/verify.py](voting-notes/code/majority-judgment/verify.py)
- [Single transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote) (Wikipedia) — the first multi-winner note here, and the one that makes the others special cases: **with one seat STV is exactly IRV**, so every center squeeze and monotonicity failure in these notes is an STV failure too. The verifier is an exact-arithmetic engine validated against the article's own published round-by-round count before being used for anything. Findings: the Droop quota is provably the smallest *safe* quota and one lower isn't (both halves checked for every electorate under 400); the quota choice is not cosmetic — Droop and Hare elect different sets 15.4% of the time, and the surplus-transfer rule matters just as much, with Scotland's WIGM and Ireland's basic Gregory disagreeing 11.7%, so "STV" names a family rather than a method. The article's own quiet admission checks out: STV and plain first-preference counting (SNTV) elect the same three of six in 61.5% of profiles, so the transfer apparatus changes the answer about two times in five. Best single illustration: the same 75 Lumen ballots elect Nguyen alone under one seat, but under three seats elect Smith, Garcia and Lee — **not Nguyen** — because "who should win" and "who should be represented" are different questions. Notes: [voting-notes/single-transferable-vote.md](voting-notes/single-transferable-vote.md), verifier: [voting-notes/code/stv/verify.py](voting-notes/code/stv/verify.py)
- [Math in Society](https://math.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Applied_Mathematics/Math_in_Society_(Lippman)) (David Lippman, LibreTexts) — the standard American gen-ed textbook treatment, chapters 2–4, and the first notes here to leave single-winner methods behind: **power indices** (Banzhaf, Shapley–Shubik) and **apportionment**. Both of its boxed impossibility theorems are overclaimed. **Arrow is stated for "a voting method"**, ordinal restriction dropped, one page before approval voting is introduced — so the chapter appears to condemn a method the theorem never reached, and the LibreTexts page still reads that way today. **Balinski–Young is stated to rule out the Alabama paradox along with the population paradox** — refuted by a method Balinski and Young built themselves, and by the book's own exercise 9, where (4,4,2)→(5,4,2) satisfies quota at both house sizes and takes nothing from anyone. Two gifts on the way past: Example 4 is the core-support argument with the arithmetic already done — the Condorcet winner holds **25.06%** of first preferences, fewest in the field — and Example 13's approval "majority failure" is manufactured entirely by the assumed cutoff, since bullet-voting the same profile elects the majority winner. Plus a Scottish Parliament example that drops a sitting MSP who turns out not to be a dummy (1/28 of the Banzhaf power), and two 1791-for-1792 date slips. - [Math and Politics Trivia](https://mathematics-democracy-institute.org/math-and-politics-trivia/) (Institute for Mathematics and Democracy, Wellesley) — 53 infographic cards in six categories, archived locally because the pages are image-only and all the text lives inside the PNGs. Gen-ed outreach pitched well below these notes, but it indexes three subjects they barely touch. **The series stopped in August 2022** and has been touched exactly once since: a December 2024 re-issue of the Population Paradox card, the only asset added anywhere in the section after 2022. That card checks out — 24 seats over states of 5,300 / 9,900 / 22,400 gives (4, 6, 14), and growing them 26% / 25% / 16% gives (3, 7, 14), so A loses a seat while growing fastest of the three — though its answer table still prints 3.57 / 6.61 / 13.81 where the stated percentages give 3.5587 / 6.5946 / 13.8467 (outcome unaffected). The apportionment sidebar advertises a **Cube Root Law** card with no content behind it, which is probably where the series died. Genuinely new relative to the glossary: **May's Theorem** (anonymity + neutrality + monotonicity ⇒ a quota method; add near-decisiveness and simple majority is forced — the one case where "which method?" has a proved answer), **Duverger's Law**, **antiplurality** (Coombs without the elimination; its own example elects B where plurality elects A), discrete cumulative voting, the Electoral College "+2 effect" and the 12th-Amendment contingent election, and the entire districting toolkit — packing and cracking, the efficiency gap, Polsby–Popper compactness, the NPVIC — which is the one major area of the subject with no entry here at all. Also **Paradox of Positive Association**, the older literature's name for monotonicity failure. Best card: Wallis's 110-ballot profile where plurality, runoff, IRV, Borda and Condorcet elect five different candidates, one each. Flags: "Fargo, MN" (North Dakota — and it stopped using approval in April 2025), "[approval] is not susceptible to strategic voting", "range voting discourages dishonest voting", and an RCV majority claim that ignores exhausted ballots. Notes: [voting-notes/mdi-trivia-cards.md](voting-notes/mdi-trivia-cards.md), cards: [voting-notes/img/](voting-notes/img/)
- [Cardinal voting systems](https://electowiki.org/wiki/Cardinal_voting_systems) (electowiki) — the first note here about the *class* rather than one method: approval, score, STAR and MJ each have a note and none of them defines the family. Its organising line is that **cardinality is a property of the grade set, not of numbers** — a cardinal ballot only out-informs a ranking when the gradations exceed the candidates, which quietly indicts a 0–5 scale in a 7-candidate field, and 0–5 is Equal Vote's own. It also supplies the half these notes were missing entirely, **cardinal proportional representation**: bloc methods, sequential reweighting, and optimal set-selection, organised by the four reweighting philosophies — **Thiele** (influence decays harmonically), **Monroe** (winners are assigned a quota of voters, who are spent), **Phragmén** (minimise the maximum load), **Vote Unitarity** (one vote's worth of influence, spent down). The party-list degenerate case is the diagnostic that makes this worth knowing: Thiele methods collapse to **highest averages** and Monroe/Vote Unitarity to **Hamilton**, the same divisor-vs-largest-remainder split [math-in-society-lippman](voting-notes/math-in-society-lippman.md) reaches from apportionment, which drags **Balinski–Young** into cardinal PR from the other direction. One of those methods, SSS, is the one whose `verbosity=0` engine path turned out to be broken in the STAR Voting library. The finding is an inference error: the page says Arrow's theorem "only applies to ordinal voting … several cardinal systems meet all these criteria." **First clause right, second doesn't follow**, three independent ways. What score and MJ satisfy is *rated*-IIA, and only while voters rate absolutely — over 200,000 random 9-voter profiles, normalised score breaks IIA **13.36%** of the time and normalised MJ **14.86%**, so the page's two named exemplars are the two methods that fail it about one profile in seven under the very normalising behaviour the same page predicts (score's conditional was already known here; **MJ breaking the same way, slightly more often, was not**). It is also a category error rather than a pass — one preference ordering admits many honest ballots that elect different people, so the rule isn't a function on Arrow's domain at all. And the escape has a price with a name: **Sen 1970 Thm 8\*2**, cardinal measurability without *interpersonal comparability* leaves the impossibility exactly where it was, and score voting is flatly not invariant under per-voter affine rescaling — the winner moved in **20.9%** of 100,000 profiles. Best of all, the article refutes itself: its own *Criticism* section records that normalisation changes outcomes "even for methods nominally passing IIA", and reports Balinski and Laraki requiring a **common language** — which *is* interpersonal comparability — two sections before presenting MJ as meeting Arrow's criteria. Pairs with the Lippman note as the same load-bearing fact dropped in opposite directions: drop "ranked" and a reader thinks Arrow condemns approval; keep it and over-infer, and they think the theorem has been beaten. Three claims left un-repeated (an uncited Bayesian-regret superlative, a STAR VSE claim cited to the simulation tool itself, and causal arguments about the majority criterion presented as exposition) — but **majority criterion for rated ballots** is worth keeping, since MJ satisfies it while failing the ordinary one, which states MJ's position more cleanly than the MJ note does. Also **random ballot** as the cardinal counterpart to Gibbard–Satterthwaite: under weak unanimity and finite precision it is the *only* strategy-proof cardinal method (Dutta, Peters & Sen 2007). ## Voting methods — theory

The notes above work examples; these work theorems. Same standard of proof — the claims are still checked by
recomputation where recomputation is possible, and the notes say plainly where it isn't.

- [Brandl & Peters, *Approval Voting under Dichotomous Preferences: A Catalogue of Characterizations*](https://www.dominik-peters.de/publications/av.pdf) (*Journal of Economic Theory* 205, 2022) — the modern companion to Brams–Fishburn 1978, and the answer to whether the dichotomous row of the approval compliance table is an assumption chosen to flatter approval. It isn't: on that domain approval is **the only rule left standing**, eight separate ways, every characterization built on consistency across variable electorates plus one headline axiom. The load-bearing fact is Inada's — under dichotomous preferences the majority relation is **transitive** and orders candidates by approval score, so Condorcet cycles cannot occur and the whole Smith/Schwartz/beatpath apparatus is idle. The centerpiece is what the *same* axiom bundles characterize on the ranked domain: consistency plus Condorcet is **impossible** with linear orders (Young–Levenglick) and jointly characterizing here, while two other bundles give **Borda** there and approval here, and two more give **plurality** there and approval here. Appendix B prices the bullet-voting collapse recorded at Dartmouth and IPO: its plurality rule keeps 5 of the 17 axioms and loses 12. The verifier confirms all 110 checkable claims including every witness the paper prints, and finds four Table 1 cells whose cited example fails a second axiom of its own theorem — all repairable from inside Appendix B, no theorem affected. One is a genuine erratum independent of transcription: **Example 8 does not satisfy the continuity it is argued to have** (P = 2{a}+2{a,b}, Q = {a,b}; f(P) = {a} but f(Q+kP) = {a,b} for every k, because Q breaks a tie among P's most-frequent ballots and the set shrinks instead of persisting). Notes: [voting-notes/brandl-peters-approval-characterizations.md](voting-notes/brandl-peters-approval-characterizations.md), verifier: [voting-notes/code/approval-characterizations/verify.py](voting-notes/code/approval-characterizations/verify.py)
- [Berg, Norine, Su, Thomas & Wollan, *Voting in agreeable societies*](http://arxiv.org/abs/0811.3245) (*American Mathematical Monthly* 117, 2010) — approval voting done as discrete geometry instead of social choice, and the only note here that never asks who wins: voters are intervals on a spectrum, and a **local** agreement condition forces a **global** guarantee. If every pair of voters shares an acceptable platform, one platform satisfies everybody (Helly's theorem at d=1); if of every three voters some two agree, some platform satisfies half; in general (k,m)-agreeability buys n(k−1)/(m−1) — though the sharper clique bound ⌈(n−ρ)/q⌉ behind that headline formula is strictly stronger 80% of the time. Checked by recomputation: Theorem 11 exhaustively over all 2¹⁵ graphs on six vertices, their Figure 7 rebuilt and their Figure 8 reconstructed as five explicit rectangles whose agreement graph is C₅ (so box societies are *not* perfect), and the restaurant example both ways. Three limits worth the space — dimension one does all the work (in ℝ² pairwise agreement stops implying a common platform), platforms are not candidates (a (2,3)-agreeable society restricted to three actual candidates drops to (2,5), halving the guarantee), and the (k,m) hypothesis is unobservable, the same move as dichotomous preferences in the approval note, except that these authors flag it themselves. Includes Burkhart's 2012 Harvey Mudd thesis, which adds a half-weight "maybe" level between approval and disapproval: its Theorem 5.1 is exact, but its headline lower bound is Abbott–Katchalski halved and never uses the second level at all. Notes: [voting-notes/agreeable-societies.md](voting-notes/agreeable-societies.md), verifier: [voting-notes/code/agreeable-societies/verify.py](voting-notes/code/agreeable-societies/verify.py)
- [Walter Horn, *Three Unique Virtues of Approval Voting*](https://www.qeios.com/read/ZETKEQ.2) (Qeios, peer-approved 2024, 3.40 from 5 reviewers) — the first advocacy *paper* here rather than an advocacy page, and the first note where the peer review is part of the finding. Its opening move is worth keeping: approval's rule can be written two ways, **"approve all and only those you approve of" (Rule 1)** or **"mark as many as you like" (Rule 2)**, and bullet voting violates only the first — which is the cleanest statement of why approval's criterion compliance is a property of the voter model. Everything after that is assumption doing the work. Virtue 1 (IIA) is true by stipulation, since Rule (1) fixes approval sets before the field is known, and it is shared with score voting under absolute scoring; virtue 3 (escapes Arrow) is shared with every cardinal method, and its supporting claim that cycles are "unavoidable under every preferentist mechanism" is false — Borda returns 3–3–3 on the standard cycle, and ranked pairs and Schulze are transitive by construction. Virtue 2 is the one with numbers, and they don't hold: the worked Powell/tax example splits **a 132-member bloc into 99 + 66**, so the printed totals 232/296/293 are an election on a 459-member House, and the "very narrow victory" of 3 votes becomes 36 or 69 once the bloc is repaired; one of those approval profiles contradicts the paper's own preference-to-approval rule; and the "generalizable" result says the option with the *most* approvals loses, which no triple can satisfy. The real finding is that the paper contains its own counterexample: enumerate all 32 sincere Rule-(1) cutoff profiles on its own ballots and **approval elects the status quo — the manipulators' outcome — in 3 of them and the Condorcet loser in 5 more**, against a 77% majority, with nobody misrepresenting anything and no agenda to set. Horn writes that sentence himself ("a very small change … would flip it") one paragraph before concluding such distortions "cannot occur under AV". Also: the agenda manipulation is defeated by plurality, Borda, IRV and pairwise majority alike, so the defence is simultaneity, not approval. Nine errata; footnote 20's combinatorics are the one non-trivial computation in the paper and are correct as printed — until you drop the three ballots its own inference rule forbids, at which point 12 scenarios become 4.
